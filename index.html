Today's report

🚨 Important change to how this page works — from 11 August. We are no longer copying the option trade. We buy the shares. The big bet is treated only as a signal that someone well-informed has taken a position. If the signal is good, the action is to buy the stock and hold it to a target set here — not to match their strike, their expiry, or their structure.

Why this matters: a long list of things that used to rule trades out simply don't apply to shares — the contract running out of time, the target being far away, the bet expiring in 2028, the fact that an option can go to zero. The bar for recommending something is now lower than it was.

What decides it now: does it genuinely look like the buyer knows something? Is the price still close to what they paid? If the stock falls, how bad can it get — because we'd be holding it. And is there something coming up that could move it? One firm rule: never buy the day before results are announced at a worse price than the original buyer got.

Each recommendation now comes with a target price and a get-out price, and no deadline — it takes as long as it takes. Downside bets (M, EXPE, GLW) can't produce a recommendation, since you can't act on them by buying shares.

    How to read this page

    The one-paragraph version. A YouTube channel called StockedUp points out unusually large options bets — someone spending hundreds of thousands or millions of dollars betting a stock will move. This page writes each of those bets down, tracks what the stock actually did afterwards, and keeps score. It is a record-keeping exercise, not a tip sheet. The whole point is to find out, over time, whether following these bets would actually have made money — and so far there isn't enough evidence to say yes.

    ⚑ What changed on 11 August. We do not copy the option trade. The big bet is only a signal that someone well-informed has taken a position. If the signal looks good, the action is to buy the shares and hold them to a target set here. That means the buyer's strike price, their expiry date and the way they structured the trade are all irrelevant to us — we're not on their clock. Most of the jargon below still explains what the original buyer did, which is how the signal is read; it no longer describes what we would do.

    Why that made the bar lower. Options expire and can go to zero, so a lot of otherwise interesting bets used to get ruled out on timing alone. Shares don't expire. What matters now is simpler: does the buyer seem to know something, is the price still close to what they paid, and how badly could this fall if we're wrong.

    Why it says "No call-to-action" nearly every day. That is the normal, expected outcome. Something only gets flagged if it clears a strict bar. Most don't. A quiet page is the system working.

    Options basics

    Option
    A contract giving you the right to buy or sell a stock at a fixed price, up to a fixed date. You pay money up front for that right. If the stock doesn't move the way you needed, the contract expires and you lose everything you paid — not part of it, all of it. That is the single most important thing on this page.
    Call
    A bet the stock will go up. It gives you the right to buy at a set price, so it pays off if the stock rises above that price. Most trades on this page are calls.
    Put
    A bet the stock will go down. It gives you the right to sell at a set price, so it pays off if the stock falls below it. On this page: M, EXPE and GLW are put trades. All three are currently losing.
    Strike
    The fixed price written into the contract — the level the stock has to get past for the bet to work. "Long $60 calls" means the buyer needs the stock above $60.
    Expiry (expiration)
    The deadline. After this date the contract is finished and worthless if it didn't work. This is what makes options unforgiving: being right eventually is the same as being wrong.
    Premium
    How much money the buyer spent. On this page it's the total size of the bet — $1.2M means someone put $1.2 million at risk. Bigger is not better: in this dataset, bet size has shown no ability to predict which trades work.
    In / at / out of the money
    In the money — the stock is already past the strike; the contract has real value today. At the money — the stock is sitting right at the strike. Out of the money — the stock still has to travel to get there. Further out means cheaper, but less likely.

    Trade shapes

    Long
    You bought it. "Long 60 calls" = someone bought calls with a $60 strike.
    Short
    You sold it. Selling an option takes in cash now in exchange for an obligation later.
    Leg
    One piece of a multi-part trade. A trade that buys one option and sells another has two legs.
    Spread (call debit spread)
    Buying one option and selling a further-away one at the same time. It costs less, but it caps the winnings at the second strike. XME: long 120 / short 140. The buyer pays less than for the 120 calls alone, and in exchange gives up everything above $140.
    Fund (ETF)
    Some rows here are bets on a basket of companies rather than one. An ETF is a single ticker that holds many stocks at once, so no individual company can sink it — but for the same reason it moves more slowly, and needs a broad trend rather than one piece of news to go anywhere. XLI holds 83 US industrial companies (Caterpillar, GE, Boeing…). XME holds 39 mining companies. Everything else on this page is a single stock.
    Volatility
    How much something typically moves. It's the yardstick for judging whether a target is realistic: a target 13% away is routine for a jumpy small-cap and a stretch for a broad, slow fund. When a note says a trade "needs a big move," this is what's being measured against.
    Why a "short leg" matters so much here
    It reveals the buyer's actual target. Nobody sells away their upside above $140 unless they think $140 is roughly where the move stops. A trade with a short leg tells you what the institution is really aiming at. A single-leg trade doesn't. That's why trades with no short leg get judged more sceptically on this page.
    Reward-to-risk (R:R)
    Most you could win versus most you could lose. 4.43:1 means risking $1 to make $4.43. Counter-intuitively this is a warning sign, not a selling point: a generous payout is the market's way of saying it thinks the target is unlikely.
    Breakeven
    Reaching the strike isn't enough — you also have to earn back what you paid. The channel never says what was paid per contract, so "the stock crossed the strike" does not prove the trade made money. This is why some wins on this page are labelled low-information.

    Badges and labels

    CALLED
    The rare case where this system actually said "this one looks worth acting on," rather than just recording it. There is exactly one: EQPT. Everything else on the page is a bet someone else made that is simply being watched.
    HIT
    The stock reached the target. For a spread that's a genuinely demanding bar. For a single-leg trade it only means the stock crossed the strike — which, as above, isn't the same as making money.
    failing
    The stock has moved substantially the wrong way and recovery looks unlikely.
    Earnings 08-12
    Amber means something is happening within about a week that could move this stock sharply.
    Resolving
    Close enough to its deadline that it will settle one way or the other shortly.
    Catalyst
    A scheduled event that could decide the bet — earnings, a regulator's decision date, a product launch. The distinction that matters: an event still ahead of the trade is meaningful; news that already came out is already in the price. A bet placed after the news isn't insight, it's momentum.
    Backfill
    An older trade added later to build up history. Never a live suggestion — its moment has passed.

    Events you'll see mentioned

    Earnings
    Every three months a company publishes its results. Stocks often jump or drop hard that day, which is why so many bets are timed around one.
    CPI
    Consumer Price Index — the monthly US inflation reading. High inflation pushes the Federal Reserve toward raising interest rates, which tends to hurt gold and mining stocks; a soft reading tends to help them.
    FOMC
    The Federal Reserve committee that sets US interest rates. Its decisions move nearly everything.
    PDUFA
    The deadline by which the FDA must decide on a new drug. A hard, scheduled date — which is why it's a textbook catalyst.
    Lockup expiry
    The date insiders are finally allowed to sell their shares. More sellers can mean a lower price.
    Beta
    How violently a stock moves compared with the market. Beta 4 means it swings roughly four times as hard — in both directions.

    The scorecard, and why it's cautious

    Tracked / Resolved
    How many bets have been recorded, and how many have finished so you can tell whether they worked.
    Decisive hits
    Only the finished trades where the outcome is genuinely clear — the stock cleared the strike by so much that the cost of the contract couldn't have mattered. Marginal wins are deliberately excluded.
    Calls issued
    How many times this system has actually said "this one looks worth acting on." It is currently one — EQPT, on 14 July 2026. Everything else here is being watched, not recommended.
    Breakeven (why "+15%" isn't the same as "winning")
    An option only makes money once the stock has moved far enough to cover what you paid for it. EQPT is the live example: the stock is up 15.1% since the bet, but the spread's breakeven is $19.96 and the stock is $19.885 — so the position is roughly back to square, not up 15%. Always judge these against breakeven, not against the entry price.
    Why the hit rate isn't trustworthy yet
    Every finished trade was entered in a short stretch of spring 2026 when almost every stock was rising sharply. In that environment, betting on things going up looks brilliant regardless of skill. Until some trades finish during a flat or falling market, the scoreboard is mostly measuring the market's luck, not the flow's insight.
    Sample size
    With six finished trades, a 2-out-of-3 result is statistically consistent with almost anything. These numbers are being accumulated toward the point where they mean something. They aren't there yet.
    Before acting on anything here

    This is a research log, not advice, and it isn't written by a licensed financial adviser. Options are among the riskiest instruments available to ordinary investors and routinely go to zero — losing 100% of what you put in is a normal outcome, not a freak one. A large institution making a bet may be hedging something else entirely, or may simply be wrong; three of the big bets tracked here are currently losing badly. Never risk money you need.
